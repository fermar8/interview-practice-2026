3. You mentioned that you're part of an on-call rotation. Could you tell me more about what that looks like? What kind of production incidents do you typically deal with, and what is your role when an alert fires?

Of course. We have hundreds of Lambda functions in AWS, each with different business impact, as well as some services running on EC2.

When an error occurs or a metric exceeds a defined threshold, an alert is triggered and a Jira ticket is created. My first step is to assess the severity and the potential business impact, and then investigate what is actually happening using CloudWatch metrics and logs.

If there is an ongoing impact, I focus first on mitigation rather than immediately trying to find the root cause. Once the immediate impact is under control, I investigate the underlying issue and either implement a permanent fix if it's critical, or create a lower-priority ticket for a later fix.

Sometimes an alert turns out to be a transient or non-actionable issue, in which case I document why and close it.

For more critical incidents, especially those with significant production impact, I work with the team on documenting the incident and, when appropriate, we conduct a postmortem to understand the root cause and identify actions to prevent the issue from happening again.


4. Can you give me an example of a production incident that you personally handled? What happened, how did you investigate it, and what was the outcome?

Sure. We started seeing errors in one of our Lambda functions that receives shipment updates through a webhook from one of our carriers.

The error indicated that a shipment tracking record didn't exist in our database. My first priority was to understand whether this was actually affecting our overall shipment-tracking flow or generating incorrect outgoing events.

I checked the affected shipment and confirmed that there was no corresponding tracking record in our database. I then looked at our data-retention and housekeeping process and found that we removed tracking records after 90 days.

At that point, I investigated the lifecycle of the shipment ID on the carrier side as well. I discovered that this particular carrier could reuse shipment IDs, and we were still subscribed to updates for some IDs after we had already removed their tracking records from our system.

So the issue wasn't really with the Lambda itself. It was a lifecycle mismatch between our system and the carrier's system: we were cleaning up our data after 90 days, but we weren't properly unsubscribing from the carrier before doing so.

I fixed this by changing the expiration logic in the Lambda responsible for unsubscribing from the carrier from 90 days to 87 days. This gives us a three-day window to unsubscribe from the carrier before our own housekeeping removes the tracking record.

The incident didn't have a production impact, so we didn't run a formal postmortem, but I documented the root cause and the permanent fix in the bug story.

The main lesson was that when integrating with external systems, we need to think about the complete lifecycle of the data on both sides, not just the lifecycle within our own system.

5. You said you first checked whether the incident was affecting the overall flow. How did you determine that there was no production impact?

I checked CloudWatch and the Lambda logs to understand exactly what was happening. In this case, the Lambda was receiving a push update from the carrier API, and our MongoDB query was returning that the shipment didn't exist.

I then checked whether there was any downstream impact. Nothing was published to our downstream consumers, and nothing was updated in our database.

I also checked whether we had previously subscribed to updates for that shipment. There was no subscribe event, no corresponding shipment in our system, and no recent subscribe operation in Splunk.

Based on those checks, I concluded that the alert itself wasn't causing any production impact. It was most likely related to the carrier continuing to send updates for a shipment that we were no longer tracking.

So at that point, I treated it as a non-actionable production alert rather than an active incident, while continuing to investigate the underlying cause.
