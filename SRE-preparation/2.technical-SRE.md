1. Imagine you're responsible for the reliability of the registration flow of a dating app. How would you define an SLI and an SLO for registration?

An SLI is the metric we use to measure the reliability of a user journey, while an SLO is the target we set for that metric.

For the registration flow, I would define the SLI as the percentage of registration attempts that successfully complete, for example:

successful registrations / total registration attempts.

Then I could define an SLO such as 99.95% successful registrations over a 30-day period.

I would also want to make sure that the SLI reflects the user's actual experience rather than just an infrastructure metric. For example, having all our Lambda functions return successfully doesn't necessarily mean that registration is working correctly from the user's perspective.

2. What happens if you consume your error budget?

It means reliability is worse than our target, so we may need to prioritize reliability work and potentially slow down changes or feature releases until reliability improves.

3. Imagine you're on call and suddenly registration success rate drops from 99.9% to 95%. You don't know the cause yet, but users are actively being affected. Walk me through what you would do from the moment you receive the alert. Hypothetical registration flow: Mobile app → Cloudflare → API Gateway → Lambda → database

First, I'd confirm that the alert is real and look at the registration SLI over time to understand when the degradation started. I'd then determine the scope: whether it's affecting all users, a specific region or platform, and whether the failures are concentrated in a particular endpoint or error type.

I'd check whether there was a recent deployment or configuration change. If there was, I'd compare the timing and changes with the incident. If there's a strong correlation and users are actively being affected, I'd consider rolling back as a mitigation rather than waiting for the root cause.

In parallel, I'd look at API Gateway and Lambda metrics and logs, checking things like 5xx errors, latency, throttling and timeouts. I'd also check the database and other dependencies to see where the failure is occurring.

Once the immediate impact was mitigated, I'd continue investigating the root cause and document the incident. If the impact was significant, I'd also make sure the relevant team members and stakeholders were aware of the incident and its status.

4. You discover that the registration Lambda is being throttled. The number of incoming requests has suddenly increased, and users are getting 5xx errors.

Lambda throttling can have several causes. One possibility is a sudden increase in traffic that causes the function to reach its available concurrency. There could also be a configured reserved concurrency limit, or we could be reaching the account-level concurrency limit.

I'd first check the Lambda metrics for throttles, concurrent executions, duration and errors, and compare them with the incoming traffic. I'd also check whether there was a recent configuration or deployment change that could have affected concurrency.

I would then investigate whether a downstream dependency is making the Lambda executions take longer than usual. For example, if we're waiting on a slow external API, each invocation stays active for longer, which can consume more concurrency and eventually lead to throttling.

If that's the case, depending on the failure mode, I might use appropriate timeouts and controlled retries with exponential backoff and jitter. I would also check other dependencies such as SQS or DynamoDB for throttling or increased latency.

For immediate mitigation, I would focus on reducing the impact first—for example, addressing an unnecessarily low concurrency limit or reducing incoming traffic if possible—while continuing to investigate the underlying cause.

5. Imagine your registration flow publishes events to SQS. Suddenly, the queue starts growing rapidly and the age of the oldest message keeps increasing. What does that tell you, what would you investigate, and what actions could you take?

A growing queue and an increasing age of the oldest message tells me that messages are arriving faster than they're being successfully processed, or that the consumers are having problems processing them.

I'd first check whether there's been a sudden increase in producer traffic. Then I'd look at the consumers: whether we have enough capacity, whether Lambda consumers are being throttled, and whether processing duration or error rates have increased.

I'd also investigate whether we're dealing with poison messages or repeated failures. For example, a consumer might be failing because of a bug or because a third-party dependency is unavailable, causing messages to be retried repeatedly.

For mitigation, if the consumers simply don't have enough capacity, I'd scale them out if possible. If there are repeatedly failing messages, I'd make sure the DLQ and redrive policy are configured appropriately so those messages don't keep consuming processing capacity.

I'd also check the SQS visibility timeout against the actual processing time. If messages are becoming visible again before processing completes, we could get duplicate processing and additional load.

For monitoring, I'd track metrics such as ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage, queue depth, consumer errors and processing latency, and I'd consider alerting on the age of the oldest message because that's a good indicator of user-facing processing delays.

Longer term, I'd investigate why the consumers are falling behind and improve their capacity, error handling, retry strategy and dependency resilience.

6. What's the difference between metrics, logs, and traces, and when would you use each one when investigating a production incident?

Metrics are numerical measurements that help us understand the health, performance and reliability of a system. They are particularly useful for dashboards and alerting. For example, we could track application errors or registration success rate and trigger an alert if they cross a defined threshold.

Logs provide more detailed contextual information about what happened inside the application. We can use them to record important events, warnings and errors, and then inspect them when investigating a specific incident.

Traces allow us to follow a particular request or transaction across different components of a distributed system. For example, if a registration request goes through API Gateway, Lambda, another service and a database, a trace can help us identify which part is responsible for increased latency or failure.

So during an incident, I'd typically use metrics to understand the overall scope and detect where something is wrong, logs to investigate what happened, and traces to understand how a specific request moved through the system and where it got stuck or failed.

"Metrics tell me that something is wrong, logs help me understand what happened, and traces help me understand where it happened across the system."

8. Why is monitoring infrastructure metrics like Lambda errors or CPU utilization not enough for a product like a dating app? What would you monitor instead?

Infrastructure metrics are important, but they don't give us a complete picture of the user experience or the business impact.

For a dating app, I'd also monitor application-level and user-centric metrics for the most important user journeys. For example, for registration I'd monitor the registration success rate and latency. For messaging, I'd look at things like message acceptance and delivery success rates, delivery latency, and potentially delayed or failed messages.

These metrics are useful because the infrastructure can appear healthy while users are still experiencing problems. For example, all our Lambda functions could have normal CPU and memory usage and relatively low error rates, but users might still be unable to complete registration because of a problem in another part of the flow.

So I'd combine infrastructure metrics, application metrics and product-level SLIs to get a more complete picture of both system health and the actual experience of the users.

Infrastructure monitoring: "Is Lambda healthy?"
Application monitoring: "Is the code behaving correctly?"
Product monitoring: "Can the user actually accomplish what they came here to do?"

9. Suppose a message-processing Lambda fails after successfully performing an operation, but before acknowledging the SQS message. The message is then delivered again. What problems could this cause, and how would you design the system to handle it

The main problem is that the message could be processed more than once, which could cause duplicate side effects.

In a distributed system, I generally try to make consumers idempotent. The implementation depends on the operation. For example, if we're writing a record to a database, an upsert or conditional write may make the operation naturally idempotent.

For an operation where duplicates are problematic, such as sending an email or creating a payment, I'd use an idempotency key and store the processing state so that repeated attempts can be detected and handled safely. If a downstream API supports idempotency keys, I'd also propagate the same key to that API.

I'd also make sure the SQS visibility timeout is appropriate for the Lambda's processing time. If processing takes longer than the visibility timeout, the message could become visible again while it's still being processed. However, visibility timeout doesn't guarantee exactly-once processing, so the consumer should still be idempotent.

Finally, I'd configure an appropriate DLQ and retry policy. For Lambda processing batches, I'd use partial batch failure handling where appropriate, so successfully processed messages don't get retried just because another message in the same batch failed.

The general principle is that with distributed systems, we should assume that messages can be delivered more than once and design our consumers accordingly.

13. Your backend is built with Node.js. You notice that a service's latency gradually increases over several hours, memory usage keeps growing, and eventually the process crashes and restarts. What would you investigate?

My first hypothesis would be a memory leak, because we're seeing memory usage continuously increase, latency getting worse over time, and eventually an out-of-memory crash. But I'd want to verify that rather than assume it.

First, I'd check whether there was a recent deployment and review the changes for common causes such as event listeners that aren't being removed, streams or resources that aren't being closed, uncleared timers, long-lived references, or an in-memory cache growing unexpectedly.

I'd also check whether we're processing unusually large database queries or third-party API responses, because retaining large objects could create similar symptoms.

In parallel, I'd look at the process and infrastructure metrics to correlate memory usage, latency, traffic and restart times. I'd also enable or inspect garbage collection metrics if available and take heap snapshots at different points in the process lifetime. Comparing those snapshots could help identify objects or references that are continuously accumulating.

If we confirm a memory leak, I'd reproduce it if possible, identify the objects preventing garbage collection, fix the underlying issue, and then monitor memory and latency over time to verify that the fix actually resolved the problem.

14. A Node.js API suddenly becomes very slow. CPU usage is close to 100%, but there isn't a corresponding increase in traffic. What could cause this, and how would you investigate it?

My first hypothesis would be that something is consuming CPU on the Node.js process, but I wouldn't assume it's a garbage collection issue immediately.

Garbage collection pressure could be one cause, especially if memory usage is also increasing. A memory leak or a large number of objects being created could cause the garbage collector to run more frequently and consume CPU.

Another important possibility is CPU-bound or synchronous code blocking the Node.js event loop. For example, a recent change could have introduced expensive computation, processing of unusually large payloads, a problematic regular expression, or some other synchronous operation.

I'd first check recent deployments and correlate the timing with the CPU increase. I'd also look at memory, garbage collection and request metrics, as well as logs around the time the problem started.

If the cause isn't obvious from the metrics and logs, I'd use Node.js profiling or CPU profiling to identify which functions are actually consuming the CPU. That would help me distinguish between GC pressure and application code blocking the event loop.

15. You're running the dating app's messaging system. Suddenly, the notification service is completely unavailable, but the core messaging service and database are healthy. Users can still send messages, and messages are being persisted successfully, but recipients aren't receiving push notifications. As the SRE, how would you respond? What would you consider a successful mitigation, and what would you monitor?

First, I would isolate the notification service failure from the core messaging flow. Since sending and persisting messages are still healthy, I would make sure the notification dependency cannot block or slow down message delivery, for example by using a circuit breaker and a durable queue.

The goal of the mitigation would be to keep message sending and persistence working normally while safely buffering notifications until the notification service recovers. I wouldn't use the DLQ as the primary buffer; I would use the normal notification queue and reserve the DLQ for messages that repeatedly fail processing.

I would monitor the notification queue depth and, especially, the age of the oldest message, as well as notification delivery success rate and latency. I would also keep an eye on the messaging API error rate, database connection pools, and overall message send success rate to make sure the outage isn't cascading into the healthy part of the system.

A successful mitigation would mean users can continue sending messages normally, no notification data is permanently lost, and we can recover and deliver the pending notifications once the notification service is back.

19. An SQS-based system processes payment events.

A consumer occasionally fails after successfully charging a customer but before deleting the SQS message.

The message is then delivered again, and the customer is charged twice.

How would you redesign the consumer to prevent this?

The first thing I would do is make the consumer idempotent, because SQS provides at-least-once delivery and duplicate messages are expected.

Each payment event should have a unique idempotency key. Before processing the payment, the consumer would atomically check or create a record for that key in an idempotency store, so two concurrent consumers cannot process the same payment.

If the key has already been processed, we skip the payment and acknowledge the SQS message. If it hasn't been processed, we perform the payment and record the result.

For an important operation like charging a customer, I would also use the payment provider's own idempotency mechanism if available. That protects us from the failure case where the payment succeeds but our consumer crashes before recording that it was processed.

I'd also configure an appropriate visibility timeout, retries, and a DLQ for messages that repeatedly fail.
